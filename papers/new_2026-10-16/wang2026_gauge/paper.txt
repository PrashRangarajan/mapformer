1. Introduction

tion. Such symmetries form high-dimensional equivalence classes in parameter space, under-
mining naive assumptions of unique optima and confounding measures like sharpness. In
Transformers, the architecture itself induces at directions along which the network and its
loss remain identical [4; 19]; these symmetries arise from the mechanism rather than from
training dynamics or regularization. Characterizing them completely is therefore necessary
for a precise theory of Transformer optimization.

chitectures.    We prove that for the canonical Transformer familyencompassing GPT-2
[15], BERT [5], and their descendantsthe gauge group of parameter transformations that

4. Architectural Extensions and Multi-Layer Structure
Having established global maximality for multi-head attention, we extend our analysis to
complete Transformer architectures. We prove that LayerNorm preserves gauge symmetries,
characterize the combined attention-FFN block structure, and establish that multi-layer
Transformers have a direct product gauge group with no inter-layer coupling.

4.1. LayerNorm Preserves Gauge Symmetry
Corollary 5 (LayerNorm compatibility) Since MHA's output is gauge-invariant and
LayerNorm acts on that tensor (Post-LN: X + MHA(X); Pre-LN: LN(X)), LayerNorm
preserves the symmetry in both variants. See Appendix C for proof.
4.2. Feed-Forward Networks and Block Structure
Feed-forward networks in Transformer blocks possess limited symmetry structure due to
non-linear activations.

Lemma 6 (FFN Gauge Structure) The gauge group of the feed-forward network with
GELU activation consists only of hidden unit permutations: GFFN = Sdf f where df f is the
hidden dimension.
     Since MHA and FFN operate on disjoint parameters and residual connections prevent
coupling, their gauge groups combine as a direct product: GBlock = GMHA × GFFN .

5. Implications for Optimization and Loss Landscape Geometry
The gauge structure has direct consequences for optimization geometry, creating at direc-
tions in the loss landscape and explaining empirical phenomena in Transformer training.

5.1. Hessian Nullspace Structure
Proposition 8 (Hessian Nullspace from Gauge Symmetry) At any critical point θ∗ ∈
Θ0 , the Hessian ∇2 L(θ∗ ) has nullspace dimension at least h(d2k + d2v ) per layer, with null
directions corresponding to the Lie algebra gmax of gauge transformations.
    Since gauge transformations preserve the network function, they preserve the loss: L(g(θ)) =
L(θ) for all g ∈ Gmax . Along any one-parameter subgroup gt = exp(tX) where X ∈ gmax ,
the loss remains constant, yielding zero curvature in these directions. For an L-layer model,
                                           2    2
the nullspace dimension is at least L · h(dk + dv ).

5.2. Optimization in Quotient Space
The gradient ∇L(θ) is orthogonal to gauge orbits at every point, so gradient descent naturally
respects the gauge without explicit constraints.   Optimization eectively proceeds on the
quotient Θ0 /Gmax of gauge-inequivalent congurations; its eective dimension is dimeff     =
dim(Θ) − L h(d2k + d2v ).
   This dimension reduction partially explains why Transformers with hundreds of millions
of parameters can be trained eectivelythe true degrees of freedom are substantially fewer
than the raw parameter count.

5.3. Mode Connectivity and Flat Minima
Critical points of Transformer training form continuous manifolds rather than isolated points.
Within each connected component of the gauge orbit, all points represent identical func-
tions with identical loss. This provides a geometric explanation for the prevalence of at
minima observed empirically and the phenomenon of linear mode connectivity between in-
dependently trained models. Two parameter congurations that dier by an element of the
                       0
identity component Gmax are connected by a at path in the loss landscape with constant
loss value; parameters related only by elements in the discrete components of Gmax (e.g.,
permutations) are not connected by such gauge-induced continuous paths.

8. Related Work
Permutation symmetries in fully connected and convolutional networks [9; 13; 14; 3] are sub-
stantially smaller than the continuous gauge groups identied here. For Transformers, van
Nierop [17] observed gauge invariance from a physics perspective without proving complete-
ness; Entezari et al. [7] studied permutation invariance for mode connectivity; da Silva et al.
[4]; Zhang et al. [19] demonstrated symmetry eects on sharpness and model fusion; and
Henry et al. [10] analyzed unnormalized attention with fundamentally dierent geometry.
Prior work on symmetry-induced at directions [12], mode connectivity [8; 6], and model
merging [1; 18] implicitly leveraged symmetries through weight matching; our maximality
proof makes those observations precise.

A.5. Technical Remarks and Contextual Notes
Remark 13 (Architectural Scope) Global maximality requires dmodel = h · dv , satised
by BERT-Base (768 = 12 × 64) and GPT-2 (768 = 12 × 64). For architectures where this
dimensional relationship does not hold exactly, continuous maximality of the gauge group is
established, though the discrete structure may dier from the pure permutation group Sh .

Remark 14 (Generic Stratum and Optimization Trajectories) The generic stratum
Θ0 where our results hold has full Lebesgue measure in the parameter space. Since optimiza-
tion trajectories are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, they avoid the
measure-zero exceptional set with probability one. Thus the characterization holds outside a
measure-zero exceptional set, not only at initialization.

Remark 15 (Essential Nature of Architectural Constraints) Several architectural choices
that appear conventional are actually mathematically essential for gauge symmetry preserva-
tion:

  1. LayerNorm placement after the output projection WO rather than before is necessary.
     If LayerNorm operated on concatenated head outputs before projection, the value-output
     transformations Ci would change individual head statistics, breaking gauge invariance.

  2. The absence of weight sharing across heads is crucial. Shared parameters would couple
     gauge transformations across heads, preventing the direct product structure.
