                                                                                      A BSTRACT
arXiv:2610.14030v1 [cs.LG] 30 Sep 2026




                                                  Looped transformers reuse one shared block across many recurrent steps, so depth
                                                  becomes a quantity that can be spent at runtime rather than a stack of distinct
                                                  layers. Those extra steps help only if a write made on one recurrent step is still
                                                  readable on later ones, and that depends on the linear map applied to the residual
                                                  between recurrent steps. We show that whether the loop learns to track state
                                                  is a geometric question about the form of this map, and we propose blockwise
                                                  conformal injections: the residual splits into coordinate-aligned groups and rotates
                                                  inside each one, so every group has its own timescale while the geometry stays
                                                  exact within it. Our experiments show a performance increase on a demanding form
                                                  of state tracking - last-token accuracy when the rest of the prefix is already correct -
                                                  and on language modelling, where a signal written earlier has to be retrieved at the
                                                  end of the sequence.



                                         1    I NTRODUCTION
                                         Transformers compute in parallel across a sequence, and that parallelism costs them depth: with
                                         a fixed number of layers they cannot, under standard complexity assumptions, recognize regular
                                         languages or decide graph connectivity (Merrill et al., 2022; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023; Chiang
                                         et al., 2023; Strobl et al., 2024). These are not exotic failures. They are the formal shadow of state
                                         tracking and of multi-step reasoning (Liu et al., 2023; Merrill et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2022), which is
                                         why depth - as opposed to width, or a short chain of thought - has become a resource worth studying
                                         on its own (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2025).
                                         One way to buy depth without buying parameters is to loop a shared block (Dehghani et al., 2019;
                                         Yang et al., 2024; Geiping et al., 2025). A weight-tied transformer unrolled to Θ(log n) depth can
                                         recognize any regular language and solve connectivity, and that is also, empirically, the depth training
                                         needs (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2025). Recurrence is the criterion that unrolling turns into: the same
                                         block can be applied at test time beyond the number of steps it was trained on (Geiping et al., 2025;
                                         Bansal et al., 2022; Schwethelm et al., 2026; Saunshi et al., 2025; Prairie et al., 2026; Movahedi et al.,
                                         2026; Deng et al., 2026).
                                         The loop still has to converge. That is a trade-off between a contraction requirement and signal
                                         preservation, and weight tying is what makes the trade-off binding. The transition operator between
                                         recurrent steps has to contract, or the loop destabilizes: that is the reason for controlling the spectral
                                         radius (Prairie et al., 2026), and for residual scaling and Jacobian regularization (Wang et al., 2026;
                                         Yang et al., 2026). The same map has to keep a signal readable across many recurrent steps, or a
                                         partial answer written on one step is gone by the step that needs it. Signal preservation, from this
                                         view, is stability of the eigenbasis of the transition operator. States at different recurrent steps stay
                                         comparable when the angles between them are preserved, up to some tolerance, and then the signal is
                                         readable along the depth axis.
                                         Angle preservation is what a conformal transformation is. The same idea is already how length
                                         generalization is obtained on the token axis, by a relative rotation (Su et al., 2024), how several
                                         timescales are kept in a Clockwork RNN (Koutnı́k et al., 2014), and how a magnitude is paired with a

These properties are the reason a loop can contract and still be read. A single shared scale gives
every direction the same lifetime, so the state is split into a few groups, each with its own rate and
its own rotation: a complex diagonal whose magnitudes are tied inside a group. Existing methods
do the opposite. Within the scope of our derivation, they rescale each coordinate by its own factor,
one direction comes to dominate the eigenbasis, and relative angles are not preserved, thereby
demonstrating that a nonnegative real spectrum is the regime that cannot express the state-tracking
problems we study (Sarrof et al., 2024; Grazzi et al., 2025).
Overcoming this structural limitation requires enforcing angle preservation explicitly by constraining
the geometry of the transition operator. In this paper, we bridge this gap by introducing a blockwise
conformal parameterization that guarantees stability along the depth axis while preserving the
integrity of the hidden state. By aligning the mathematical requirements of signal preservation with
the architecture of looped transformers, we enable robust, long-horizon recurrence at test time.
Our contributions are

    • a theoretical characterization of the destabilizing factor of leaving the eigenbasis of the tran-
      sition operator unconstrained in the recurrent form of a looped transformer, together with a
      parameterization that constrains it and keeps the hidden state readable across any number of
      recurrent steps - a pathway toward test-time scaling that is not capped at the training trip count;
    • improved last-token accuracy on state-tracking word problems, where the model must emit the
      correct final product given that the prefix is already correct;
    • improved language-model quality when a signal has to reach the end of the sequence, with lower
      validation perplexity and higher LAMBADA, so the same linear transition operator helps on
      text as well as on the synthetics.


2    R ELATED W ORK
Looped and recurrent-depth transformers. Weight tying with a variable trip count goes back to the
Universal Transformer (Dehghani et al., 2019) and has become an axis of test-time compute: k layers
looped L times match kL distinct layers on iterative reasoning and can simulate chain-of-thought
steps in latent space (Saunshi et al., 2025); an adaptive trip count generalizes in length on copying,
parity and addition (Fan et al., 2025); a 3.5B recurrent-depth model keeps improving on GSM 8 K out
to tens of loops (Geiping et al., 2025); and looped models at 1.4B and 2.6B trained on trillions of
tokens reach the quality of much larger fixed-depth models (Zhu et al., 2025). Depth allocated per

token rather than per model is a parallel line (Bae et al., 2025; Bansal et al., 2022), and on the theory
side Θ(log n) looped depth suffices to recognize regular languages and is what training appears to
need (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2025).
Parcae. Prairie et al. (2026) treat the recurrent state as a discretized linear recurrence and inject the
prelude embedding at every pass through a diagonal transition operator with entries in (0, 1), which
bounds the spectral radius below one and makes long trip counts stable. Residual scaling, Jacobian
regularization and related stabilizers pursue the same end by other means (Wang et al., 2026; Yang
et al., 2026; Wang & Reid, 2026), and equilibrium treatments take the fixed point as the object of
interest (Anil et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2026; Movahedi et al., 2026; Fein-Ashley & Rashidinejad,
2026). All of these constrain the magnitude of the transition operator. Prairie et al. (2026) note that
many forms of the injection would work and choose a nonnegative diagonal because it is simple and
cheap; that remark is the opening this paper takes up.
Test-time generalization across recurrent steps. The Universal Transformer adds a sinusoid of
the recurrence index to the state (Dehghani et al., 2019), and Xu & Sato (2025) condition per-loop
residual scales and normalization gains on an additive frequency embedding of the loop index. An
additive index tells the model which recurrent step it is on, and at the same time it restricts what the
model can do: the embedding is tied to the trip counts seen in training, so the loop cannot freely
extrapolate past that range. On the token axis, rotary embeddings (Su et al., 2024) were the first to put
a relative rotation into the residual geometry; later work generalizes the planes (Zhang et al., 2025) or
accumulates Householder reflections along the sequence (Yang et al., 2025). Those maps are built
per token and applied once.
Structured orthogonality and linear recurrences. The LRU and diagonal state-space models
introduce a complex eigenbasis, with a magnitude and a phase per plane (Orvieto et al., 2023; Gu
et al., 2022). We use that same notion, applied to the recurrence that buys the model test-time compute
rather than to the sequence axis. Vorontsov et al. (2017) show that an orthogonal parameterization
improves performance, and also that a hard constraint is not required: a neighbourhood of the
orthogonal family trains even better. That softer neighbourhood is the same tendency as a Clockwork
RNN (Koutnı́k et al., 2014; Mikolov et al., 2015) - several timescales instead of one - and it is the
tendency we keep when the state is split into groups. Jing et al. (2017) build a unitary from layers of
2 × 2 rotations and find that a small capacity is preferable on copying. Block-diagonal orthogonal
factors appear in parameter-efficient finetuning (Liu et al., 2024; Gorbunov et al., 2024), on weight
matrices rather than on a recurrent transition operator.
Normalization and privileged bases. Ashkboos et al. (2024) establish that an RMSNorm residual
stream is exactly invariant to an orthogonal change of basis, so a rotation applied to the state is
invisible to the normalizer that follows it. Elhage et al. (2023) document that the residual stream
nonetheless develops an approximately axis-aligned privileged basis, attributable to per-coordinate
optimizer normalizers. Together: rotating the residual stream is free as far as the norm is concerned,
while the coordinate frame is not an arbitrary choice - two facts that matter when we place structure
in that stream.

3     M OTIVATION OF R EADABILITY
The block of a transformer is nonlinear, and that is where its expressivity lives. In a loop the same
block is applied at every recurrent step, and the cost of that expressivity is how much of a signal
present at the start of the recurrence is still readable at the end. Between two steps the residual passes
through one linear map, the transition operator Ā, and the block that follows is the same map every
time. Whatever that block computes, it computes as an inner product: attention logits, a linear readout,
the pre-norm rescaling. The transition operator therefore decides whether those inner products still
match the ones the block would have computed when the signal was written. Proofs are in Section C.

3.1   A R EADABLE O PERATOR IS A C ONFORMAL O NE
Definition 3.1 (readable). A matrix M ∈ Rd×d is readable if there is a c > 0 with ⟨M x, M y⟩ =
c2 ⟨x, y⟩ for all x, y ∈ Rd .

The block compares states by inner products, so a state stays readable when any two vectors can
still be compared after the operator. The definition says that directly: for every pair x, y, the inner

product of M x and M y is the inner product of x and y, up to one scale shared by the whole state.
The comparison of x with y is the comparison it was. An operator that treats directions differently
fails the test, because that same pair no longer presents the same comparison. That is what a later
recurrent step needs if it is to read a state written on an earlier one, and it is the property the transition
operator has to stabilize.
Proposition 3.2 (readability is conformality). M is readable with constant c if and only if M = cQ
with Q⊤ Q = I.

Readability, read strictly, is conformality: the transition operator preserves the angles between
eigenvectors. We write Arot = cQ for such a map.

3.2   W EIGHT T YING I S A C URSE FOR R EADABILITY
Rotary embeddings (Su et al., 2024) put a rotation on the token axis and apply it once. On the depth
axis the same kind of map is applied to its own output at every recurrent step, and repeated application
of a linear map is power iteration.
Proposition 3.4 (iteration collapses a non-conformal operator). Let Ā be normal with eigenvalues
λj , and let E be the eigenspace of largest magnitude. If Ā ∈ CO(d) then ∥Āt u∥ = ct ∥u∥ for every
u, and Āt u/∥Āt u∥ = Qt (u/∥u∥) moves on the unit sphere without converging. If instead the largest
magnitude is strictly dominant, then dist(Āt u/∥Āt u∥, E) → 0 geometrically for every u with a
nonzero component in E.

A transition operator that scales directions differently does not only fade a write. It turns every write
toward one subspace, so writes from different recurrent steps end up pointing the same way. A
conformal transition operator does not: the direction keeps moving, and the block can still tell the
steps apart. Readability at one step propagates to every later step, and the distortion between those
steps is estimated by a cosine, one for every pair of depths. For u ̸= 0 let

5.4   T HE T RAINED M AP H AS THE P REDICTED G EOMETRY

We further measure this geometry on the language models trained above. The Toeplitz defect asks
whether two appearances of one state, a fixed number of recurrent steps apart, still present the block
with the same comparison wherever in the loop they sit. That constancy is what depth invariance
makes desirable: extra recurrent steps then do not rewrite the geometry the block reads. Section B
defines the defect and states the proposition that a vanishing value certifies conformality, and that a
nonzero value certifies the operator is not conformal. Table 5 is the measurement. The conformal
method stays within precision error at every width and every depth. The diagonal method does not:
the defect grows with the number of recurrent steps, and it is already larger at larger width, from 0.06
at 60M and T =8 toward 0.3 by T =32. That growth is the collapse of Proposition 3.4, and it meets
Table 4 where the diagonal perplexity stops responding to further recurrent steps.


6     C ONCLUSION

We presented a blockwise conformal map between recurrent steps of a weight-tied transformer. The
need it answers is state tracking on the depth axis, with a potentially very large number of virtual
layers obtained by repeating the same block across more recurrent steps. Those layers count only
while a signal written early is still readable late. The map between steps has to contract, or the loop
does not settle, and it has to keep the angles between states, or the tied block can no longer compare
what it wrote. Left free, one direction comes to dominate as the same map is applied again and again,
and the comparisons collapse. Conformality, or a neighbourhood of it, is the practical way through:
the state shrinks together, the angles stay, and a few groups are allowed their own pace, so part of
the signal outlasts the rest. That is what turns a long unroll into computation rather than a fade. The
gains are on an extreme form of state tracking, the word problem in a finite group, and the same map
also improves language modelling. The direction worth working on is to spend that depth. Larger
models, run for many more recurrent steps than training currently affords, are where a readable loop
should keep using layers it was never shown.

A.4    O N I MPORTING S PECTRAL O BSTRUCTIONS
The spectral results we invoke in Section 3.3 are theorems about the token-axis recurrence of a linear
RNN, and we are explicit about what they license. The negative direction is what rules out the
baseline: a nonnegative real spectrum cannot recognize PARITY at arbitrary length (Sarrof et al., 2024,
Thm. 2), and Grazzi et al. (2025, Thm. 1) show an eigenvalue off the nonnegative reals is necessary,
with a non-real one required for counting modulo m (Grazzi et al., 2025, Thm. 2). Freeing the phase
in (4) removes that obstruction.
The positive direction does not transfer as cleanly, and we do not claim it does. What those results
certify as sufficient for composing a non-commutative transition monoid is a transition that depends
on the input, realized as a product of generalized Householders (Grazzi et al., 2025; Siems et al.,
2025, Thms. 3-4), and a fixed transition operator iterated T times is a statement about powers of
one matrix. Our claim on A5 and S5 is empirical: the shape of the transition operator changes what
gradient descent finds, at matched spectra and matched everything else. Making θ a function of the
token would bring the construction inside the sufficiency results, costs nothing in the implementation
(Section D), and is the extension we would run next.

A.5    N ORMALIZATION
Pre-normalization is what makes Corollary 3.3 operational. Ashkboos et al. (2024) show that an
RMSNorm residual stream is exactly invariant to an orthogonal change of basis, which is the property
(1) needs: a global normalizer removes the shared scale and commutes with Q. No global normalizer
can undo a per-coordinate change of angle, and the angular part is what Section 3 constrains.

# --- inside the loop: x <- Block(A_bar x + dt * B e), one tied block per pass ---
def core_block_forward(self, x, input_embeds, freqs_cis, mask, step, total_steps, **kw):
    x = self.transformer.adapter(x, input_embeds)      # the transition operator, below
    for block in self.active_blocks:                   # the tied recurrent block
        x = block(x, freqs_cis, mask, **kw)
    return x

def forward(self, x_t, e):                             # the injection module
    return self._apply_A(x_t, self._decay()) + self._dt() * (e @ self.B.T)

def _apply_A(self, x, decay):
    return self._rotate(x) * decay                           # ours: rotate, then group scale

# --- rate and angle: c_g = exp(-softplus(gamma_g) softplus(dt_g)), theta_i = omega_i dt_g ---
def _group_expand(self, v):                            # (G,) -> (d,), one value per group
    return v.repeat_interleave(2 * self.pairs_per_group)

def _decay(self):
    gam, dtg = F.softplus(self.gamma_log), F.softplus(self.rot_dt_log)               # (G,), (G,)
    return self._group_expand(torch.exp(-gam * dtg))   # one modulus per group

def _theta(self):
    dt_g = F.softplus(self.rot_dt_log)                 # decay and phase share one timescale
    return self.rot_omega * dt_g.repeat_interleave(self.pairs_per_group)

# --- the transition operator itself: blockdiag(R(theta_1), ..., R(theta_P)) as a pairwise
     complex multiply ---
def _rotate(self, x):
    theta = self._theta().to(x.dtype)
    cos, sin = torch.cos(theta), torch.sin(theta)
    xr = x.reshape(*x.shape[:-1], self.rot_pairs, 2)
    x0, x1 = xr[..., 0], xr[..., 1]
    return torch.stack([x0 * cos - x1 * sin,
                        x0 * sin + x1 * cos], dim=-1).reshape(x.shape)
