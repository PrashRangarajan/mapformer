MapFormer × PoPE — positional encodings on language

Position codes
on language

Two recent papers change how a transformer represents where a token is. MapFormer replaces the token’s index with a learned running sum, built for navigation; PoPE separates what a token is from where it is. This work tested both on language-like data — bracket languages, real code, text and music — and re-checked MapFormer’s navigation results at the length they were trained on. The clearest results: on bracket languages path integration keeps an advantage that extra depth cannot buy when brackets nest deeper than in training; PoPE’s encoding improves MapFormer; and MapFormer’s own low-rank bottleneck is what makes its navigation solution hard to find.

The short version

  1. Path integration beats ordinary positions on Dyck-2 when brackets nest deeper than in training, and depth doesn’t close that gap. +0.17 at four layers, 8/8 seeds, at the training length but three times the training depth; standard models plateau around 0.77 there. At the training depth itself, extra layers do close most of it (+0.29 at one layer, +0.02 at four).
  2. PoPE’s encoding also improves MapFormer. At the training length the combined model beats MapFormer on every task where PoPE helps and is never detectably worse — so when a task needs path integration, add PoPE on top. Where position only counts tokens, plain PoPE (music) or plain RoPE (code) is better.
  3. MapFormer’s own bottleneck limits it. Trained and tested on long navigation walks, the paper’s rank-2 design finds the solution on 2 of 8 seeds and rank 4 on 8 of 8 — though rank 2 can represent and hold that solution. It is a problem of finding it, and it is in the paper’s design.
  4. Three published results replicate: MapFormer on Dyck-2, and PoPE on both Indirect Indexing and Bach Chorales.
  5. A 48-parameter fix matches a 786k-parameter one. A simple distance penalty in attention repairs long-context failures on Bach, and improved every model on real code.
  6. A simple data trick beats PoPE’s published Bach score by a wide margin — 0.394 against their 0.489.
  7. The metric the MapFormer paper uses on Dyck is unreliable. A model with no memory scores 0.88 on it, and it reports the effect running in the opposite direction to what it actually does.

Path integration on Dyck-2, at unseen nesting depth

bracket language · width fixed, depth varied · 4 models × 8 seeds × 4 depths, one batch

Dyck-2 is the language of balanced brackets, ( ) [ ]. To predict which closer is legal, a model must track a stack whose depth goes up on every opener and down on every closer — a signed quantity, which is exactly what a running sum represents and a token counter cannot. The natural objection is that a deeper standard transformer can learn to track the stack through attention alone. So width was held fixed and only depth varied:

  • 8/8 seedsDepth closes about 40% of the gap, then stops. Standard models stop improving after two or three layers; three and four layers are indistinguishable (+0.159 vs +0.168). Path integration buys something extra depth does not.
  • ScopeIt is an effect at unseen nesting depth. Length, width and batch are matched and a no-memory floor is reported, but the headline depth is three times the training depth. At the training depth, depth nearly closes the gap — so what path integration buys here is robustness to deeper nesting than it was trained on.
  • ScopeIt is a stack-tracking effect, not a long-context one. At four times the training length the advantage disappears from three layers up.

It agrees in direction with MapFormer’s own navigation result, measured at the training length: with a converged training recipe, path integration beats a matched index model by +0.243 (8/8; 0.971 against 0.805, floor 0.506). The two tasks share the property that makes a signed running sum useful — position that can move backwards.

Three replications

published results reproduced from scratch

  • ReplicatesMapFormer on Dyck-2, on the paper’s own metric: MapFormer beats a two-layer RoPE by +0.370 (paper +0.44), 8/8. The ordering replicates (+0.064 on the more reliable closing-accuracy metric below); the paper’s absolute scores come in 0.015–0.112 lower here.
  • ReplicatesPoPE on Indirect Indexing — a pointer-arithmetic task — at 200k training steps, where it solves the task on 7 of 8 seeds. At their stated 100k it does not (1/8): training sits on a plateau and then drops suddenly at a point that varies from 65k to 157k steps between seeds, so the budget decides the outcome.
  • ReplicatesPoPE on Bach Chorales: PoPE beats RoPE by 0.032 NLL, 5/5, against their published 0.019.

Our PoPE implementation was also checked directly against the authors’ released code and matches to 1.7×10−6, so the comparisons above test PoPE as published.

Other positive findings

cheap fixes that outperformed elaborate ones

A 48-parameter fix for long contexts

Models trained on short sequences often break when run on longer ones. A distance penalty in attention — one learnable number per head, 48 in total — repairs this on Bach Chorales as well as a 786k-parameter alternative does:

Test NLL, lower is better, 5/5 seeds for each repair. On real code the same fix improved all four models on every seed, even at the training length; plain RoPE plus the fix scored best of the eight code models, though its lead over the next ones is not statistically established.

A simple data trick beats the published Bach score

  • 5/5 seedsTransposing the music by a few semitones during training — which PoPE’s paper applies to its other music dataset but not to Bach — improves PoPE by 0.107 NLL, over three times the 0.032 gap that separates PoPE from RoPE. Augmented PoPE reaches 0.394 against their published 0.489.

Path integration trains faster on pointer arithmetic

  • DirectionalOn Indirect Indexing the path-integrated model tends to reach the solution sooner — earlier on 6 of 7 paired seeds, median 70k steps against 100k — though the size of the speed-up is not statistically established. Given enough steps both reach the same end point, so this is about how fast it learns, not how well.
  • CaveatIt is also three times more robust to extra padding (0.430 vs 0.149 when examples are padded to a longer block, 6/7 seeds). Likely a robustness effect rather than a capability one, and not yet controlled.

How the two ideas combine

adding PoPE’s encoding to path integration, and the other way round

MapPoPE puts PoPE’s way of using the angle on top of MapFormer’s path-integrated position. Measured task by task, PoPE’s encoding carries over: wherever it helps an ordinary model, it also helps a path-integrated one.

  • DetectableAdding PoPE’s encoding to path integration is a good idea at the training length. Detectably better on three tasks, never detectably worse on any. Beyond the training context it can collapse — on Bach MapPoPE scores 4.616 against MapWM’s 1.397 — unless the 48-parameter fix above is added (0.622). If a task calls for MapFormer, use MapPoPE with the fix.
  • DetectableAdding path integration to PoPE is not, on clock-like tasks. Where position only counts tokens, plain PoPE wins: a learned counter is slightly but consistently worse than the exact one the index provides for free.
  • In practiceThe two ideas are not symmetric partners. PoPE’s encoding reliably helps a path-integrated model where it helps at all, but on its own it is not a general improvement: it wins on music, is small on Dyck, ties RoPE on code (and scores worse on 3/3 seeds once both have the distance fix), and loses on the navigation index model. Path integration is a specialist tool for where the angle comes from — worth it when position is genuinely signed, as in navigation and bracket structure, and not otherwise.

A navigation check: MapFormer’s own bottleneck

torus navigation · trained and tested on 1024-step walks · 3 models × 8 seeds · identical starting weights except the bottleneck

MapFormer turns each token into a step through a small bottleneck — the paper uses rank 2, arguing that a 2-D world needs a 2-D step. An earlier result here favoured rank 4, but only on walks longer than those trained on, which is exactly the kind of result the measurement lesson below distrusts. So it was re-run at the training length, with every model built from the same starting weights and differing only in the bottleneck:

  • 8/8 vs 2/8It is the paper’s own design that struggles. The per-head version has the same four latent dimensions in total as rank 4; what differs is how many drive each head’s angle. That number decides it.
  • ExistenceRank 2 can represent and hold the solution — it just rarely finds it. Squeezing a trained rank-4 model to rank 2 keeps 0.995 accuracy; trained further from there, rank 2 keeps it on 7 of 8 seeds, like rank 4. So the problem is search, not capacity.
  • ScopeOne task, one width, a fixed budget. Rank 4’s output layer also starts at a smaller scale, which is not yet separated from its extra dimensions.

What this says about measurement

findings that apply beyond these models

The Dyck metric in the MapFormer paper runs the effect backwards

The paper scores Dyck-2 with F1 valid continuation. On that scale a model with no memory at all scores 0.884, higher than most real models, because two-thirds of positions only need the previous token. And on the same trained models it reverses the shape of the effect:

metrictraining length4× lengthsays the effect
closing accuracy, chance 0.5+0.357+0.136falls 2.6×
F1 (the paper’s metric)+0.075+0.306rises 4.1×

F1 heavily penalises probability placed on illegal tokens, and ordinary models place more there as sequences grow — so F1’s gap widens for a reason unrelated to tracking the stack. The paper’s “helps out of distribution” pattern on Dyck is produced by the metric.

  • MethodTrain at the length — and depth — you test at. Every claim here that one design is better and held up was measured at the training length. Testing past the training length measures something different — how gracefully a model degrades on lengths it never saw. That is worth having, as the 48-parameter fix shows, but it is not the same as being a better model. Many published positional-encoding comparisons are measured the second way.
  • MethodAveraged metrics favour the more local model. Six cases, always the same direction. Bits per byte, F1 and any average over all positions are dominated by easy tokens, so when they disagree with a metric conditioned on the hard case, the average picks the model better at local prediction.
  • MethodA task’s floor predicts, before any training, whether an experiment can work. On real Python a no-memory model already gets 0.858 of brackets right and 99.7% of brackets close within 8 levels, so a stack test on code could never discriminate — and it did not.
  • MethodPoPE’s advantage over RoPE is large only on its own dataset, and that dataset is limited by overfitting — the augmentation result above is 3× the effect it is used to demonstrate. Elsewhere it is small (Dyck, +0.01 to +0.03) or absent (code).
  • MethodThree seeds cannot settle small effects. The common “effect larger than 2.8·sd/√n” rule is a 10.7% false-positive test at three seeds, not 5%; small code differences quoted at n=3 are therefore not established.

What didn’t hold up

kept here so none of it gets re-cited

  • RetractedA large advantage on real code at long lengths disappeared once models were trained at the length they were tested at — the source of the “train at the length you test at” lesson above.
  • RefutedThree proposed explanations — that it works on code because code nests, that PoPE extrapolates because its magnitudes can’t go negative, and a clock-versus-map rule predicting when PoPE helps — each failed its own registered test. (Describing positions as signed or counting still stands; the prediction built on it did not.)
  • CorrectedTwo headline numbers were too big. Navigation’s +0.461 came from an under-trained recipe (+0.243 converged), and the Dyck advantage was first described as at the training length when it is at three times the training depth.
  • Withdrawn“Rank 2 loses because its code is skewed” and “the rank gap is just training speed” — replaced by the matched test above.
  • Retracted“Extra depth replaces path integration” came from a comparison that changed width along with depth; the fixed-width result above supersedes it.

A consistent pattern across the week: controlled interventions reproduced every time; proposed explanations of why did not. The results on this page are the interventions.
